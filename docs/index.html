About and method

NISAR's L-band radar, scored against an optical burn map, over the 2026 peat fires in Central Kalimantan, Indonesia. The timeline sets every radar pass beside every optical pass of the season.

What the layers are

HV and HH
The radar sends a horizontally polarized pulse. HH is the part of the echo that comes back horizontal, HV the part that comes back vertical. Leaves and branches turn the wave, so standing vegetation is bright in HV. When it burns, the HV echo drops. Over peat swamp HH changes too, but in these windows it does not follow the burn. A likely reason, not tested here, is that HH mostly follows how wet the ground is.
Radar change
The after image minus the before image, in decibels, averaged to about 100 m for display. The scores use cells of about 200 m.
Fire hotspots
Centres of VIIRS 375 m active-fire detections from NASA FIRMS. A hotspot is a detection of heat at overpass time, not a map of burned ground.
Optical burn map
Change in the Normalized Burn Ratio between July 2026 and the few usable passes from 4 October to 5 November 2026, from Harmonized Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS). The after side is read through smoke, and where no clear pixel exists the map is grey and nothing is scored.

Limits

  • Two windows of about 22 km, two orbits, one fire season. This is not a burned-area product.
  • The optical map used as truth is itself read through smoke. Thick smoke lowers near-infrared reflectance, so some of the optical burn may be haze.
  • Large parts of both windows have no clear optical pixel in the after period and are not scored.
  • The 1 dB rule finds the strongly changed core of the burn, not all of it. It was fixed before scoring and has not been tuned or cross-validated.
  • In window 2 the placebo test fails on some pairs: before the fires, cells that later burned were already losing HV (AUC up to 0.77), and the burned sample is small. Window 1 is the clean case.
  • Radar change is not fire by itself. Water level, rain and land clearing change the echo too, and one such patch shows in window 1 between 4 and 16 July.
  • The camera did see the ground: on most passes in August and early September, and once per window at the peak of the fires. The start of the peak period (15 September) was chosen after looking at the images, which is why all three periods are shown. The optical images are surface reflectance, already corrected for the atmosphere, so thin haze looks clearer than it was.
  • The radar products are mostly provisional releases. The display images use a 3 by 3 mean filter; the scores use no speckle filter beyond averaging to 200 m.
